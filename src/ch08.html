The honest-gap formula

Four parts, about 30 seconds

  1. State the fact plainly, with no apology and no exaggeration: what you have done and what you have not.
  2. Show adjacent experience that is true.
  3. Show that you understand the topic: the concepts, and how you would approach it.
  4. Show what you are doing about it, with a concrete and recent action.

“I haven't shipped X to production. I've done Y, which is related. My understanding is …, and here is how I would approach it. I'm currently …”

DoDon't
Be precise about your role: “I contributed to”, “I led”, “I attended”Claim ownership of work you only observed
Keep numbers consistent, such as years of experience, across your CV, profile and conversationRound up, or contradict yourself. Interviewers cross-check.
Say “I don't know, but here is how I would find out”Bluff. One probing follow-up exposes it and costs trust everywhere else.
Move on to your strengths after answeringOver-apologise or dwell on the gap

Why it works: senior interviewers expect gaps. They are testing judgement, honesty and how fast you learn. “I haven't done that, but here is how I would approach it” scores better than a bluff that gets caught.

1. Data structures and algorithms

Typical gap: a moderate problem-solving level. Prepare by practising the patterns in Chapter {{ch:6}} and explaining complexity out loud.

If asked about your level

“Algorithms aren't my daily work. Android architecture and performance are. I've been practising patterns deliberately: hash maps, two pointers, sliding windows, trees and dynamic programming. I can reason about trade-offs and complexity, and I talk through my approach as I go.”

How do you perform well in the round?

  • Clarify, then give an example, then state the brute force with its complexity, then optimise, code, test, and state the complexity again.
  • Think aloud. Interviewers score your reasoning, and a hint is part of the conversation. Accept a hint gracefully and build on it.
  • If you are stuck: restate the problem, try a tiny example by hand, ask whether sorting or a hash map would simplify it, and deliver a working brute force rather than nothing.
  • Name the pattern you see, such as “this looks like a sliding window, because we want the longest contiguous range”.
  • Never claim a solution is optimal without justifying its complexity.

Honesty point about experience: state your years of experience consistently everywhere. If asked, give the real figure and describe what you have done, not an estimate that changes between answers.

2. Accessibility (deep dive)

Typical gap: no production accessibility work. Prepare with TalkBack, semantics, content descriptions and focus order.

Honest answer if asked “What accessibility work have you done?”

“I haven't owned an accessibility initiative in production. I know the model well. TalkBack reads the semantics tree, so I make sure every actionable element has a meaningful label, role and state, a logical focus order, 48 dp touch targets, sufficient contrast, and support for large font sizes. I verify that with TalkBack and Accessibility Scanner. Here is what I have done in practice: [name one true example, such as a content description you added or a screen you tested at large font sizes]. I'm now doing an accessibility pass on a sample app to build hands-on experience.”

Core concepts to know cold

  • TalkBack is Android's screen reader. You explore by touch: swipe right or left to move to the next or previous item, and double-tap to activate. Other services include Switch Access, Voice Access and BrailleBack, plus display settings such as font size and colour correction.
  • Accessibility services do not see pixels. They read the accessibility node tree for Views and the semantics tree for Compose, which carry the label, role, state and actions.
  • Principles (WCAG-style): perceivable, operable, understandable, robust. Body text contrast should be at least 4.5:1, or 3:1 for large text and UI components. Touch targets should be at least 48 × 48 dp. Do not rely on colour alone. Support 200% font scale by using sp. Avoid time limits without a way to extend them.

Content descriptions

  • Label every meaningful non-text element, such as icon buttons and informative images. Mark decorative elements as ignored, using contentDescription = null in Compose or importantForAccessibility="no" in XML.
  • Describe the purpose, not the picture. Write “Delete order”, not “trash icon”. Do not add the word “button”, because TalkBack announces the role. Localise labels through string resources.
  • Prefer visible text as the label, since Compose merges it automatically. Text fields need a label or hint that stays available while typing.
Icon(Icons.Default.Delete, contentDescription = stringResource(R.string.cd_delete_order))
Image(painter, contentDescription = null)                  // decorative: skipped by TalkBack
<ImageButton android:contentDescription="@string/cd_share" android:minWidth="48dp" android:minHeight="48dp" />

Semantics in Compose

  • Modifier.semantics { } edits the semantics node. mergeDescendants = true groups children into one focusable item, so a row with an icon, a title and a subtitle reads as one sentence. clickable and toggleable merge automatically. clearAndSetSemantics replaces everything.
  • Expose the role, state and actions: role = Role.Button, stateDescription, onClick(label = "…"), customActions to replace hidden swipe gestures, heading() for section titles so TalkBack can jump between headings, and liveRegion for dynamic updates.
Text("Orders", Modifier.semantics { heading() })
Switch(checked, onCheckedChange, Modifier.semantics {
    stateDescription = if (checked) "On" else "Off"
})
Row(Modifier.clickable(onClickLabel = "Open order", onClick = onOpen)) { Thumb(); Title(); Price() }
Box(Modifier.semantics { liveRegion = LiveRegionMode.Polite }) { Text(statusText) }

Focus and navigation order

  • The default traversal follows layout order: start to end, top to bottom. Fix the order through structure first, then use overrides: isTraversalGroup and traversalIndex, or focusProperties { next = … } in Compose. In XML, use accessibilityTraversalBefore and accessibilityTraversalAfter, or nextFocusDown.
  • Keyboard, D-pad and Switch Access need every control to be focusable and operable without touch gestures, with a visible focus indicator. For custom views, use AccessibilityDelegateCompat to set the role and actions.
  • Moving focus on change: after opening a dialog or screen, or on an error, move focus to the title or the first error with a FocusRequester. Use live regions for status messages, and make dialogs trap focus.
  • Do not build custom gestures such as swipe, long-press or drag without an accessible alternative action.

How do you test it?

  • Manually: turn on TalkBack and complete the key journeys with the screen off. Try 200% font and display size, Switch Access, and a keyboard.
  • Accessibility Scanner, plus the Layout Inspector accessibility checks: touch target size, contrast and missing labels.
  • Automated: Espresso AccessibilityChecks.enable(), Compose accessibility checks in UI tests, semantics assertions such as onNodeWithContentDescription and assertHasClickAction, the lint ContentDescription rule, and screenshot tests at large font scale.
  • Process: include accessibility in design reviews and in the definition of done. Automated checks catch the mechanical problems, but not whether the experience makes sense.

Typical follow-ups: “How would you make a custom chart accessible?” (a text summary with semantics, or an alternative data table). “A list row has an image, a title, a subtitle and a delete button?” (merge the row, and keep delete as its own action or a custom action). “How do you announce a snackbar?” (a live region, or the built-in announcements). Trap: labelling everything blindly creates noisy output. Less, clearer and logically ordered is better.

3. Formal design reviews

Typical gap: architecture discussions are routine, but formal review ownership is not confirmed. If you have a genuine example, prepare it.

If asked “Tell me about a design review you led or took part in”

“Most of my design discussions happened [in pull requests, in team sessions, or informally]. Here is a real one: …” Describe your actual role precisely. If you have never run a formal review, say so, give the closest real example, and explain how you would run one.

Structure a genuine example (STAR): Situation (what needed designing), Options that you or the team considered, Your contribution (a question you raised, an alternative you proposed, a risk you found), Outcome (the decision and what changed), and Lesson.

What does a formal review look like?

  • Artifact: a design doc, RFC or ADR with context, goals and non-goals, options considered, the proposal, trade-offs, risks, rollout and rollback, metrics and open questions.
  • Process: the author circulates the document in advance, comments are gathered asynchronously, contested points go to a review meeting, the decision is recorded, and a revisit trigger is set.

Questions a good reviewer asks:

  • What problem does this solve, and what happens if we do nothing?
  • What are the failure modes, such as offline use, partial failure, retries and data loss? How do we roll back?
  • How does it scale in data, users and team size, and what does it cost in build time, app size and battery?
  • How is it tested, observed in production, and how do security and privacy apply?
  • What is the migration path, and who owns it after launch?

Receiving feedback: separate the design from yourself, ask for the concern behind each comment, and record decisions and their reasons.

How do you build real evidence now?

  • Write a short ADR, such as docs/adr/0001-use-room.md, for a real decision in one of your projects. It becomes a concrete talking point.
  • Review pull requests with a design lens: boundaries, naming and testability. Write down what you would flag.
  • Ask a colleague to review one of your design documents, then describe how their feedback changed it.

Trap: inventing a formal review story, or describing someone else's work as your own. If you cannot name a concrete decision and its trade-offs, the story is not ready.

4. Advanced system design

Typical gap: architecture experience is solid, but unfamiliar large-scale systems may not be. Prepare trade-offs, scalability and component boundaries, building on Chapter {{ch:7}}.

Component boundaries: the senior lens

  • A good boundary has one reason to change, a small public API, and hides its data and technology choices. Depend on abstractions across the boundary.
  • Data ownership: each module owns its tables and state. Other modules go through its API. No shared mutable globals.
  • Communication: direct calls for tight cohesion, events or flows for decoupling, and navigation contracts between features.
  • Smells: a change in A always requires a change in B, circular dependencies, a “common” or “utils” module that everything imports, and DTOs or database entities leaking across layers.

Worked example: an offline-first chat client

  • Requirements to clarify: one-to-one or group chats? Media? Read receipts? History size? Multiple devices? Latency target?
  • Boundaries: :feature:chat for the UI and ViewModel, then :core:chat-data for the repository and sync, then :core:database (Room) and :core:network (REST and WebSocket). Notifications arrive through FCM.
  • Data flow: the UI observes Room, and the local database is the source of truth. Sending inserts the message locally with a client-generated ID and state SENDING. A sync worker or WebSocket sends it, and it becomes SENT, then DELIVERED or READ. On failure it becomes FAILED and can be retried.
  • Ordering and deduplication: the server assigns a sequence number per conversation. The client ID makes retries idempotent. After a reconnect, fetch everything since the last sequence number to fill gaps.
  • Transport: WebSocket while the app is in the foreground, for low latency. FCM wakes the app in the background to sync. REST with a Paging 3 RemoteMediator loads history.
  • Media: uploads run through WorkManager, which is resumable and respects constraints. Show thumbnails first, and cache with size limits.
  • Failure modes: reconnects with backoff and jitter, duplicate delivery, out-of-order arrival, the app being killed mid-send (the outbox survives in Room), and clock skew.

WebSocketReal-time and bidirectionalBattery use, reconnect logic, killed in the background

Practice prompts (45 minutes each, out loud)

  • An image feed with infinite scroll and an offline cache · A photo or video uploader that can resume · Offline maps and downloads
  • A notification system with channels, grouping and delivery · A video player with prefetching · A checkout or payments flow with idempotency and security
  • A reusable Android SDK with a public API, binary compatibility and size limits · Real-time collaborative editing with conflict resolution

Drill: 5 minutes on requirements, 10 on the high-level design, 20 on the deep dive into sync, concurrency and failures, and 10 on trade-offs and evolution. Record yourself and listen for “it depends” without the “on what”.

Trap: naming technologies instead of reasoning. Always finish with: what breaks first at 10 times the scale, how I would detect it, and what I would change.
